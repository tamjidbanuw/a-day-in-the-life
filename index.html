Meet the World

How do you measure a day? Most countries count people, money, and borders. Only 35 countries have asked thousands of ordinary people to record every minute of an ordinary day.

We often compare countries by their wealth, population, or geography. But there is one thing every person receives equally: time. Across these 35 countries, thousands of people kept detailed time diaries, recording how they spent every minute of a normal day. Together, these countries represent more than half of humanity. This story follows them — not because they are the whole world, but because they reveal how an ordinary day is lived across it.

    Chapter Two

    The Only Number That Asks

    Every chart so far measured what people did. This one asks what they feel. And somehow, that answer follows patterns we can measure.

    A simple question

    Happiness isn’t measured.
    It’s answered.

    Imagine a ladder. The top rung is the best life you can imagine. The bottom is the worst. Where would you place yourself today?

    Thousands of people in every country answer that single question. Their average becomes the country’s happiness score. Unlike every other measure in this story, happiness isn’t observed. It’s self-reported. That makes it the most human number on this page.

    Life satisfaction
    0 to 10

    A surprising pattern

    Feelings follow the money…
    more than you’d expect.

    If happiness were purely personal, countries would scatter randomly across the chart. They don’t.

    As income rises, happiness usually rises with it. The relationship isn’t perfect. Some countries are happier than their wealth predicts. Others are less happy despite their income. Money doesn’t explain everything. But it explains far more than many people expect.

    One discovery

    No Country Wins Everything

    We often imagine there must be one country that has figured life out. There isn’t.

    Across four measures — sleep, work, leisure, and happiness — every leader eventually gives up first place. Mexico works the longest. France sleeps the most. Norway enjoys the most leisure. Finland reports the highest happiness. Four different countries, four different strengths. No single nation leads them all.

    Every line follows one country across four rankings. When lines cross, countries trade places. The lesson isn’t who finishes first — it is that nobody stays there. Ranks are by size, not by merit: 1st on Work is the longest working day, not the best one. 34 countries, not 35: Luxembourg keeps a time diary but has no happiness score. Time use: OECD Time Use Database, both sexes. Happiness: World Happiness Report, 0–10 ladder.

    Chapter Three

    One Country Writes Everything Down

    Until now, every country has been reduced to five numbers. One country lets us open the entire day — every minute, every activity, nothing left hidden.

    A closer look

    What happens inside 1,440 minutes?

    The United States isn’t shown because it’s typical. It’s shown because it’s unique. Among the countries in this story, it is the only one that publishes time diaries in enough detail to rebuild an entire day. For everyone else, the day ends as five numbers. Here, we can open every minute.

    An ordinary American day
    Every minute included

    This is one complete day reconstructed from more than 170,000 real time diaries. Nothing has been removed. Every minute belongs somewhere.

    From five activities…
    to forty-four

    The five broad categories you’ve seen throughout this story unfold into 44 everyday activities. Sleeping. Working. Driving. Cooking. Watching television. Reading. Playing. Everything adds back to exactly 1,440 minutes. Bars are scaled within their own block, not across the day. Sleeping is 8h40 and commuting is 17 minutes, so one shared scale would draw most of these as a hairline. These totals are not the same as the OECD figures for the United States elsewhere on this page — ATUS counts travel as its own activity where the OECD folds it into whatever the travel was for. Read the shares within this section, not across sections.

    Same hours.
    Different lives.

    Everyone is handed the same 1,440 minutes. What changes, from Osaka to Mexico City to Rome, is what becomes of them.

    Every person receives the same 1,440 minutes. What changes is how those minutes become work, family, rest, opportunity, and connection. Somewhere in these thirty-five countries, someone is living a day that looks remarkably like yours. The question is—which life would you choose?
