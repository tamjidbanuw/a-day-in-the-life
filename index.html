Chapter One

How We Live

Sleep, work, rest — three demands every day makes, and no two countries meeting them the same way.

The day

Everyone gets 24 hours —
almost no one spends them alike

How those hours divide has less to do with the clock than with where you wake up.

Two lives

The same day,
a world apart

Set two countries side by side and the gaps stop reading as numbers. They start reading as two different lives.

VS
Work

Half your waking hours
go to work — some of it unseen

Every day carries two workloads: the job you're paid for, and the cooking, cleaning and caring that no one puts on a payslip.

Count both and Mexico works the longest day of anyone here, ten hours; Canada comes nearest, though far more of its day is on the clock. Italy has the shortest paid day measured, under three hours, and still does more unpaid work than paid.

Rest

The other half of the day —
and who guards it

After the work is done, what's left is rest: the hours for family, friends, screens, sport and simply doing nothing.

Germany guards the most, 5h41m a day, with Italy close behind. Mexico is left with 3h23m, its hours already committed. The countries that work longest rest least — which is arithmetic more than character.

Chapter Two

How We Thrive

Effort is supposed to pay off. The numbers are less obedient than that.

Thrive

A longer day doesn't
buy a better life

If effort alone decided it, the busiest countries would be the healthiest and the happiest. They are neither.

Japan lives longest, 83.9 years, yet comes tenth of twelve for happiness. Australia is the happiest here on a working day barely shorter than Japan's — the difference is how little of it is paid. India works a day within minutes of Japan's and finishes last on both counts. How a day feels answers to more than how full it is.

Chapter Three

How We Connect

Different days, and still a steady traffic of people going to see how everyone else manages theirs.

Connect

Whatever the day looks like,
the world comes to visit

Hundreds of millions of people cross a border every year to see somewhere else's ordinary.

France alone takes nearly 218 million visitors a year, more than three times the number of people who live there. One country's routine is another country's reason to travel.

Chapter Four

What It Adds Up To

One pattern runs underneath all of it, and it isn't the one you would guess.

The pattern

Same wealth.
A completely different day.

Richer ought to mean busier, or else freer. It reliably means neither.

Canada and Germany earn almost exactly the same per person. A Canadian works nearly two hours more each day; a German takes those two hours as leisure. Same pay, different life.

Two countries, near-identical wealth

What it comes down to

✦

What a country earns says remarkably little about how its people spend their hours.

✦

Money sets the ceiling. Habit and choice decide what actually fills the day.

Chapter Five

Your Closest Match

Every country keeps its own balance of time, health, community and connection. So do you.

Find your match

Somewhere out there,
a country lives like you

The twelve countries are scored on four measures — time, health, community and connection. Set the dials to your own life and we'll find the country that comes closest.

The four scores are an illustrative index built from the same sources used throughout: daily free-time share (OECD Time Use), life expectancy (Our World in Data), a community score combining social support, freedom and generosity (World Happiness Report), and international arrivals (World Bank). Each is normalised 0–100 across the twelve countries, and is meant for exploring rather than ranking.

Same hours.
Different lives.

Everyone is handed the same 1,440 minutes. What changes — from Osaka to Mexico City to Rome — is what becomes of them.

Work or rest. Paid or unpaid. Alone or together. How a country fills its day shapes how long its people live, how connected they feel, and how content they are to be here. Whose day would you want?
