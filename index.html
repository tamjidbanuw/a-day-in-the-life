Meet the data

8 Billion People

Measuring a day takes a national diary survey. Thousands of people write down what they did and for how long. Only 35 countries run one.

This story follows the 12 with complete records. Between them they hold 3.82 billion people, 48% of everyone alive. Nine are high-income countries. The other three, India, China and Mexico, are not. Read this as a window on rich and emerging economies, not the world.

The second shift

Half your waking hours
go to work

Every day carries two workloads: the job you're paid for, and the cooking, cleaning and caring that no one puts on a payslip.

Count both and Mexico works the longest day here, 10h05m. Canada is next at 9h02m, though more of its day is paid. Italy has the shortest paid day measured, 2h57m, and still does more unpaid work than paid.

Off the clock

The other half of the day

After the work is done, what's left is rest: the hours for family, friends, screens, sport and simply doing nothing.

Germany keeps the most free time, 5h41m a day. Italy is close behind at 5h23m. Mexico is left with 3h23m. The countries that work longest rest least.

Chapter Two

How We Thrive

Effort is supposed to pay off. The numbers are less obedient than that.

Diminishing returns

A longer day doesn't
buy a better life

If effort alone decided it, the busiest countries would be the healthiest and the happiest. They are neither.

Japan lives longest at 83.9 years and ranks 10th of 12 for happiness. Australia is the happiest and works 8h01m, just 12 minutes less than Japan. The difference is paid work: 3h58m against 6h08m. India works 8h06m and finishes last on both counts.

Chapter Three

How We Connect

Different days, and still a steady traffic of people going to see how everyone else manages theirs.

Inbound

The world comes to visit

Hundreds of millions of people cross a border every year to see somewhere else's ordinary.

France takes 217.9M visitors a year, more than three times the number of people who live there. One country's routine is another country's reason to travel.

Chapter Four

What It Adds Up To

One pattern runs underneath all of it, and it isn't the one you would guess.

Same wallet

Same wealth.
A different day.

Richer ought to mean busier, or else freer. It reliably means neither.

Canada and Germany earn within $71 a head of each other, $54,848 against $54,777. A Canadian works 1h46m more each day. A German takes 2h10m more leisure. Same pay, different life.

Fig 7.1 — Find Your Match Nearest-neighbour match on four normalised scores. Your answers place you in the same 0–100 space as the 12 countries; the closest by straight-line distance wins. Illustrative, not a ranking.

The four scores are an illustrative index built from the same sources used throughout: daily free-time share (OECD Time Use), life expectancy (Our World in Data), a community score combining social support, freedom and generosity (World Happiness Report), and international arrivals (World Bank). Each is normalised 0–100 across the twelve countries, and is meant for exploring rather than ranking.

Same hours.
Different lives.

Everyone is handed the same 1,440 minutes. What changes, from Osaka to Mexico City to Rome, is what becomes of them.

Work or rest. Paid or unpaid. Alone or together. How a country fills its day shapes how long its people live, how connected they feel, and how content they are to be here. Whose day would you want?
