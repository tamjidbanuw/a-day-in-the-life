Sample · not wired into the story

The chapter already sitting
in the data folder

The story counts a country’s day as one day. The survey behind it counted two, and the second one has been in the repo the whole time.1

Every figure on the live page is the Total row of time_use_oecd.csv. Beside each of those rows sit a Male row and a Female row, for all 35 countries the file covers, including all twelve the story follows. Read them and the day stops being a national average and starts being an arrangement between two groups of people — one the survey finds doing more of the work that pays, the other more of the work that doesn’t.

Three charts below, all the same shape: one country per dot, men along the bottom, women up the side, and a diagonal where the two would be equal. The shape is doing the argument. A dot’s side of the line is the finding; the distance from it is the size of the finding.2

Leisure, minutes per day
Men horizontally, women vertically. Every dot below the diagonal is a country where men get more free time.

Nobody Is Above The Line Leisure in minutes per day, men against women, 35 countries. The diagonal is parity; dashed lines mark an hour either side of it. Red marks the twelve countries the story follows.

Data source: OECD Time Use Database, via data/time_use_oecd.csv in this repository.

Note: Leisure as the survey defines it — sport, socialising, television, reading, hobbies. It excludes sleeping and eating, which are counted as personal care, and it excludes the travel between activities.

Thirty-five countries, and not one of them puts a dot above the line. That is the whole finding, and the chart makes it in about a second: an empty upper triangle is harder to argue with than the sentence “men have more leisure in every country measured”. The average gap is 44 minutes a day.

Unpaid work, minutes per day
Cooking, cleaning, shopping, care.

The Other Empty Triangle Unpaid work in minutes per day, men against women. The same 35 countries, the same axes, and now the empty half is the other one.

Data source: OECD Time Use Database.

Note: Unpaid work is household and care work done for one’s own household or for others without pay. Voluntary work is included; commuting is not.

Paid work, minutes per day
All employment, averaged over everyone.

Where The Payslips Are Paid work in minutes per day, men against women. Averaged across the whole adult population, not only those in a job.

Data source: OECD Time Use Database.

Note: Averaged over everyone surveyed, employed or not, so a country with fewer women in work shows it here as a smaller daily average rather than as a separate employment rate.

Put side by side they look like a trade, and the obvious next thought is that it cancels: one group does the paid work, the other the unpaid, and the days come out even. The third chart adds the two together and checks.

All work, paid and unpaid, minutes per day
The two previous charts summed. Above the line, women’s total day of work is longer.

It Does Not Cancel Paid plus unpaid work in minutes per day, men against women, 35 countries. The four below the line are the only places measured where men’s combined working day is the longer one.

Data source: OECD Time Use Database. Sum of the paid and unpaid work series shown above.

Note: Surveys were run in different years, mostly between 2009 and 2016, and a few are older. This is a portrait of 35 countries, not a single moment, and not a trend.3

It does not cancel. In 31 of 35 countries the combined working day is longer for women: 8h05m against 7h33m on average. The trade is real, but it is not an even one, and the half-hour left over is roughly the leisure gap the first chart opened with.

The four exceptions are worth naming, because they are not the ones a reader would guess and they are not flukes of measurement either: Denmark, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Norway. Even there the margin is 4 to 10 minutes — parity, not reversal. None of the four is among the twelve countries the story follows, which is the honest caveat: the countries where this evens out are missing from the live page entirely.

Notes

  1. The file is 525 rows: 35 countries × five activity measures × three sex categories. The story reads only the 175 rows where Sex is Total. ↩
  2. A parity scatter answers “who does more” without a single number in the prose, but it can only compare two things at once, which is why paid and unpaid work need two charts rather than one. ↩
  3. Time-use surveys are expensive and run rarely, so no international collection of them is contemporaneous. Comparing a 2016 survey with a 2009 one assumes the arrangement moved slowly. Over seven years, for this measure, that is a defensible assumption rather than a safe one. ↩
