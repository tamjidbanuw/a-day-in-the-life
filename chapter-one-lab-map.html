The day, mapped

One square per country, placed roughly where it belongs. Each square is that country's 24 hours, read top to bottom. Hover one and it lifts out of the world while the rest recedes.

Not a chart shape you have seen before, because it is two things at once: a map and 35 stacked days. Pick a measure and every square keeps only that band at full colour, so the geography of the finding appears — unpaid work is a southern and eastern European story, paid work an East Asian one, and sleep is the same everywhere, which is the chapter's whole point.

Thirty-five days, in place
Hover a square to open it. The row it belongs to lights up below.

The Day, Mapped One tile per country, positions schematic rather than projected — no geometry is used, so no country is weighted by its area on a globe. Each tile is a stacked day: 24 hours read from the top of the square to the bottom. Every tile is the same size, which means Luxembourg's day counts for as much as China's. The rows beneath are the same 35 days as 48 counted half-hour cells, allocated by largest remainder so each row sums to exactly 48. Cell and band order is fixed and is not a timeline — the data records how much, never when. OECD Time Use Database, both sexes.

Buys: a geographic read the story has never had, an unfamiliar object that still resolves into something readable, and a hover that is worth doing — the tile is deliberately too small to read until you open it, which is what makes opening it feel like finding something.

Costs: a tile map has to be learned; the first two seconds are "what am I looking at". Only 35 of ~195 countries exist here, so the empty space is not "no data", it is "not in this story", and the caption has to carry that. And schematic placement will irritate anyone who knows where Croatia is.
